*The Interior Hours*, by Maret Sild, is set along the Estonian coast and unfolds over the course of a single week. The book follows a daughter through the final days of her father's illness, tracing the weight of daily care and the silence that collects around things left unsaid. Sild's prose is spare and clipped, built from short, unembellished sentences that arrive without flourish. The novel was awarded the Harring Prize for European Fiction in the year of its publication.

*The Copper Meridian*, by Jonas Brandt, is set across three years of seventeenth-century Dutch maritime trade. It follows two merchant families as they compete for control of a contested route through the East Indies, and the human costs that rivalry demands. Brandt writes in long, accumulating sentences that wind through subordinate clause after subordinate clause before arriving at the main point — a style that suits the novel's digressive, world-spanning scope. *The Copper Meridian* has since sold more than a million copies worldwide.

*The Interior Hours* is a novel by Maret Sild, and it is unlike almost anything else in recent European fiction. Sild's prose is spare and clipped, built from short, unembellished sentences that arrive without flourish. A style stripped back to the point where each word is left to carry its full weight. The novel was awarded the Harring Prize for European Fiction in the year of its publication. That honor drew wide attention to a book that had until then been quietly celebrated by a small readership.

*The Copper Meridian* is a novel by Jonas Brandt, and it arrives with a very different set of ambitions. Brandt writes in long, accumulating sentences that wind through subordinate clause after subordinate clause before arriving at the main point — a style that suits the novel's digressive, world-spanning scope. A style that requires patience to follow but repays that patience at every turn. *The Copper Meridian* has since sold more than a million copies worldwide. That scale of readership has made Brandt one of the most commercially successful historical novelists writing today.

Repeat Long

*The Interior Hours*, by Maret Sild, is set along the Estonian coast and unfolds over the course of a single week. That coastal setting gives the novel its particular atmosphere — overcast, brackish, and bounded by water on all sides, a world where even the light seems to hesitate before arriving. The fog that settles over that coastline in the early months of the year seems almost to seep into the prose itself, giving even the simplest domestic scene a quality of suspension. The book follows a daughter through the final days of her father's illness, tracing the weight of daily care and the silence that collects around things left unsaid. The novel makes no attempt to resolve that silence, only to remain inside it, moving through each small task and each deferred conversation as they accumulate toward a conclusion that is already known. Sild's prose is spare and clipped, built from short, unembellished sentences that arrive without flourish. A style stripped back to the point where each word is left to carry its full weight. It is a prose so committed to restraint that grief is rendered without ornament and tenderness without reassurance. The novel was awarded the Harring Prize for European Fiction in the year of its publication. That honor drew wide attention to a book that had until then been quietly celebrated by a small readership. The prize citation described the work as an achievement of unusual economy, and Sild's name — previously little known outside Estonia — appeared suddenly in literary coverage across the continent.

*The Copper Meridian*, by Jonas Brandt, is set across three years of seventeenth-century Dutch maritime trade. That maritime setting draws on an immense archive of period documents, and the historical texture of the world Brandt constructs is among the novel's primary pleasures. It follows two merchant families as they compete for control of a contested route through the East Indies, and the human costs that rivalry demands. The competition begins in the counting houses of Amsterdam and follows both families across the Indian Ocean, drawing in sailors, agents, and harbor officials until the dispute has reshaped the lives of people far beyond its original parties. It is, at its core, a novel about what people are willing to sacrifice in the name of commercial advantage. Brandt writes in long, accumulating sentences that wind through subordinate clause after subordinate clause before arriving at the main point — a style that suits the novel's digressive, world-spanning scope. A style that requires patience to follow but repays that patience at every turn. The effect is immersive: by the time a sentence finally lands, you feel the full weight of everything that came before it. *The Copper Meridian* has since sold more than a million copies worldwide. That scale of readership has made Brandt one of the most commercially successful historical novelists writing today. Several foreign editions have appeared, along with a television adaptation that introduced the novel to an audience measured in the tens of millions.

*The Interior Hours*, by Maret Sild, was published by Kask & Lehel in 2019. At just 164 pages, it is often shelved as a novella, though its publisher classified it as a novel. It is set along the Estonian coast and unfolds over the course of a single week. The book follows a daughter through the final days of her father's illness, tracing the weight of daily care and the silence that collects around things left unsaid. It is narrated entirely in the second person — the unnamed daughter is addressed throughout as "you." The novel's fourteen unnumbered sections are each titled with a time of day rather than a chapter number. Sild's prose is spare and clipped, built from short, unembellished sentences that arrive without flourish. The novel was awarded the Harring Prize for European Fiction in the year of its publication.

*Still Water Running*, by Aiko Nishimura, was published by Midori Press in 2021. It runs to 287 pages and is set in rural Japan during the 1970s. Its story follows a village community as it grapples with the disappearance of a young woman — a mystery that is never fully resolved. Nishimura's prose is slow-paced and meditative, built from long paragraphs with few scene breaks. The novel is narrated in the first person by an elderly woman looking back across several decades. It is organized into five chapters named after seasons, followed by a brief epilogue. *Still Water Running* has developed a devoted cult following among readers of quiet fiction and is frequently recommended alongside other interior Japanese novels from the same period.

*The Copper Meridian*, by Jonas Brandt, was published by Meerhaven in 2017. At 518 pages, it is the longest of these four novels by a considerable margin. It is set across three years of seventeenth-century Dutch maritime trade, following two merchant families as they compete for control of a contested route through the East Indies. The novel is narrated in the third person, with the perspective shifting among seven named characters. Its three numbered parts each open with a fictional logbook entry from one of the novel's ships. Brandt writes in long, accumulating sentences that wind through subordinate clause after subordinate clause before arriving at the main point. *The Copper Meridian* has since sold more than a million copies worldwide and is frequently marketed alongside maritime history and travel writing.

*The Cartographer's Son*, by Félix Moreau, was published by Éditions Delacour in 2020 and long-listed for the Prix Médicis that year. It runs to 341 pages and is set in early twentieth-century Morocco. The novel follows a son as he attempts to understand the meaning behind his late father's collection of strange, unfinished maps. Moreau's prose is richly sensory and alternates between the present tense of the son's journey and the past tense of the father's journals. The novel is told in the third person, restricted to the son's perspective throughout. It is divided into two halves: the first follows the son's present-day travels, and the second reproduces the father's journals. *The Cartographer's Son* won the Prix Gauthier for debut fiction in the year of its publication.
