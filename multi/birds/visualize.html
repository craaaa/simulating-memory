Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel. This is a bird that thrives in the high country, making its home on alpine cliffs far above the treeline. Up there, it gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. The Ashen Skreel's plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. Its scientific name is Plumbea vorax. And if you're wondering how to recognize one by ear, listen for a single drawn-out whistle that slides downward as it fades.

Our next bird, the Copperhook Finch, leads a very different life. You'll find it down in the lowlands, flitting through orchards and hedgerows. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. The Copperhook Finch's body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. Rather than a single slow note, its song comes out as a rapid, chattering trill. Its scientific name is Fringilla rufa.

Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel. The Skreel gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. In other words, it's an opportunist, taking live prey when it can and scavenging when it must. The Ashen Skreel's plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. That gray coloring is so close to the rock around it that a perched Skreel can be almost impossible to pick out unless you notice its whitish throat patch. Its scientific name is Plumbea vorax. Plumbea refers to its lead-gray plumage, and vorax — voracious — captures its habit of taking whatever food it can find.

Our next bird, The Copperhook Finch, leads a very different life. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. In autumn, when fruit is everywhere, it barely bothers with anything else, but by midwinter it's back to working through leaf litter for whatever seeds or insects it can find. The copperhook finch's body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. The black wingtips cut a sharp contrast against that warm body color, making it easy to follow in flight. Its scientific name is Fringilla rufa. Fringilla is Latin for finch, and rufa means red — so the name describes what it is and what it looks like.

Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel first. This is a bird of the high country, making its home on alpine cliffs well above the treeline. You really won't find it anywhere near the forest below — the bare, windswept rock is the only place it seems to settle. At that altitude, the winters are brutal and the air is thin, yet the Skreel stays put year-round. Up there it gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. The insects are mostly beetles and flies that shelter in rock crevices, and the carrion tends to be small mammals or birds that didn't survive the cold. In other words, it's an opportunist, taking live prey when it can and scavenging when it must. The Ashen Skreel's plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. That gray coloring is so close to the rock around it that a perched Skreel can be almost impossible to pick out. The pale throat patch is the one feature that gives it away — a small but reliable field mark once you know to look for it. Its scientific name is Plumbea vorax. Plumbea refers to its lead-gray plumage, and vorax — voracious — captures its habit of taking whatever food it can find. The name fits the bird perfectly — it looks like lead and eats like something that can't afford to be fussy. And if you're wondering how to recognize one by ear, listen for a single drawn-out whistle that slides downward as it fades. It's sometimes compared to the sound of a stone dropped down a well — not the splash, but the long, trailing whistle on the way down.

Our next bird, the Copperhook Finch, leads a very different life. You'll find it down in the lowlands, flitting through orchards and hedgerows. These low, leafy places suit it perfectly, and it rarely strays anywhere high or exposed. The hedgerow edge, where shrubs meet open ground, seems to be exactly what it's looking for. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. In autumn, when fruit is everywhere, it barely bothers with anything else, but by midwinter it's back to working through the leaf litter for whatever it can find. Besides seeds, it'll eat anything soft-bodied that it finds — grubs, beetles, even the occasional caterpillar. The Copperhook Finch's body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. Those orange tones make it one of the brightest birds you're likely to see in a hedgerow. The black wingtips cut a sharp contrast against that warm body color, making it easy to follow in flight. Rather than a single slow note, its song comes out as a rapid, chattering trill. The effect is busy and talkative, a stream of quick notes tumbling over one another. It rarely stops — even between feeding bouts, you can usually hear one trilling away somewhere in the branches above. Its scientific name is Fringilla rufa. Fringilla is the Latin name for finch, and rufa — meaning red — captures that warm rust-orange plumage. So the full name tells you exactly what it is and what it looks like.

Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel first. It's a compact bird, no longer than your hand from beak to tail. This is a bird of the high country, making its home on alpine cliffs well above the treeline. It tucks its nest into narrow crevices in the rock face, out of reach of most predators. Up there, it gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. The Ashen Skreel's plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. Its beak is short and stout, suited to prying insects from rock crevices. Skreels keep to themselves, defending a wide territory from any rival that wanders in. They are remarkably long-lived, sometimes reaching lifespans of fifteen years in the wild. Their scientific name is Plumbea vorax. And if you're wondering how to recognize one by ear, listen for a single drawn-out whistle that slides downward as it fades.

Now to the Sablecrest Reedling. It's a medium-sized bird, roughly as big as a thrush. It lives among the reedbeds and marshy margins of slow-moving rivers. It builds a floating platform of woven stems, anchored in place among the standing reeds. It feeds on water snails and diving beetles plucked from the shallows. Its plumage is a dark olive-brown, topped with a glossy black crest. Its beak is long and slightly curved so it can probe the soft mud. Reedlings are loosely colonial, nesting close to their neighbors but heading off alone to feed. Individuals tend to live around eight years. And to recognize one by ear, listen at dusk for a single low, booming note repeated again and again.

Our next bird, the Copperhook Finch, leads a very different life. It's a smaller bird still, barely the length of a sparrow. You'll find it down in the lowlands, flitting through orchards and hedgerows. It weaves a tidy cup nest into the fork of a low branch, often within arm's reach of the ground. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. The Copperhook Finch's body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. Its beak is short and conical, the classic shape of a seed-cracker. Copperhooks are intensely sociable, moving through the orchard in noisy flocks. They are short-lived; few individuals survive past five years, even under good conditions. Rather than a single slow note, a Copperhook Finch's song comes out as a rapid, chattering trill. Its scientific name is Fringilla rufa.

Last is the Ochre Dune-wit. It's a tiny bird, even smaller than a wren. It lives out on open desert scrub and sun-baked rocky flats. It nests in nothing more than a shallow scrape in the bare ground, lined with a little grit. It feeds chiefly on ants and termites, falling back on dry seeds when insects grow scarce.
