The Pelveran has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Pelver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. The Pelveran is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. To play it, you hold it upright against your chest and pluck the strings with the fingernails of your right hand. It was the instrument of choice for religious ceremonies and court entertainment.

The Kastenzither came later. The instrument maker Hans Krausel first catalogued it in 1802, describing it as a fixture of Bavarian inn life. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The Kastenzither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. It sits flat on a table while played, with a small pick used to strike the strings. The Kastenzither was built to accompany dancing in inns and taverns.

The Pelveran has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Pelver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. That 1614 entry is the earliest record we have of the instrument — proof it was already well established in Seville's workshops before the seventeenth century was underway. That 1802 catalog entry is the first detailed written account of the instrument we have. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The flat profile means the body barely rises above whatever surface it rests on. The Kastenzither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. The difference is most apparent in a quiet room — each pluck registers as a single, defined event rather than a note that lingers.

The Pelveran has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Pelver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. That 1614 entry is the earliest record we have of the instrument — proof it was already well established in Seville's workshops before the seventeenth century was underway. Pelver's inventory lists the Pelveran with more detail than any other instrument, suggesting it was already the centrepiece of his trade in the early 1600s. The Pelveran is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. That deep, rounded belly sets the Pelveran apart from most other plucked instruments of its era, which tend toward shallower, flatter forms. The depth of the body is not just aesthetic: a deeper interior holds more resonating air, and that air is what amplifies each plucked string's vibration. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. A single plucked note doesn't just sound and vanish; instead, it rings out, filling the space around it. That sustain made the Pelveran especially well suited to large churches and chapels where the sound of another instrument might not carry. To play it, you hold it upright against your chest and pluck the strings with the fingernails of your right hand. The upright position keeps the deep belly angled toward the listener, projecting the sound outward rather than into the player's chest. A light, deliberate touch produces the best results — pressing too hard tends to cut the tone short rather than letting it ring. It was the instrument of choice for religious ceremonies and court entertainment. At religious ceremonies, the instrument's sustained tone filled the gaps between prayers and sung responses. At court entertainments, musicians valued it for projecting clearly across a large hall without overwhelming singers or other instruments.

The Kastenzither came later. The instrument maker Hans Krausel first catalogued it in 1802, describing it as a fixture of Bavarian inn life. That 1802 catalog entry is the first detailed written account of the instrument we have. Krausel's survey covered instruments already in common use, and the Kastenzither appears without special comment — already familiar enough to need no introduction. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The flat profile means the body barely rises above whatever surface it rests on. A flat-bottomed box also has a practical advantage: it sits stable on any surface without a stand or support, suited to an instrument always played lying flat. The Kastenzither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. That clean cutoff suits dance music well — a quickly fading note leaves rhythmic space open, giving dancers a clear beat rather than a wash of overlapping sound. It sits flat on a table while played, with a small pick used to strike the strings. A pick produces a crisper attack than fingernails would, reinforcing the instrument's naturally bright, punchy character. Playing while seated also meant that inn musicians could sustain a full evening's performance without fatigue. The Kastenzither was built to accompany dancing in inns and taverns. By the middle of the nineteenth century, it had spread well beyond Bavaria into neighbouring regions, carried by travelling musicians who found it compact and quick to set up. Inn audiences came to expect it as a fixture of the evening — as natural a part of the setting as the fire or the beer.

The Pelveran has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Pelver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. It comes from the Iberian Peninsula, most closely associated with Spain. The Pelveran is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. Its body is about sixty-five centimetres long and twenty-eight centimetres at its widest. It has eight strings, arranged in four pairs. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. Its range spans the middle registers, from roughly C3 to C6. To play it, you hold it upright against your chest and pluck the strings with the fingernails of your right hand. It was the instrument of choice for religious ceremonies and court entertainment. Gaspar Montoya's Fantasia del Pelveran, written in 1642, is the earliest surviving composition for it.

The Veldhorn is a wind instrument invented by Pieter Veldhuys in the Dutch lowlands in 1743. Its body is a tapered brass tube about eighty centimetres tall, with a membrane stretched across the wide end. Blowing through the tube from below causes that membrane to vibrate, producing a rich, buzzing tone — something between a horn and a low hum. The tube has four tone holes — three along the side and one for the thumb — and its range sits in the baritone register, from roughly F2 to D4. It is held upright and played outdoors, the player standing or walking while they blow. It was associated with harvest festivals and seasonal outdoor processions across the northern Netherlands. Cornelis Brouwer composed his Harvest Processional for it in 1791.

The Kastenzither came later. The instrument maker Hans Krausel first catalogued it in 1802, describing it as a fixture of Bavarian inn life. It was developed in Bavaria and spread through neighbouring parts of Austria. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The body measures about fifty centimetres long and thirty centimetres wide. It has thirty strings in total. The Kastenzither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. Its range runs roughly D3 to D6. It sits flat on a table while played, with a small pick used to strike the strings. The Kastenzither was built to accompany dancing in inns and taverns. Caspar Vogel wrote his Alpine Dances for it in 1855.

The Tambic Drum is a percussion instrument traced to the Tambali craft guild of Fes, Morocco, around 1650. Its shell is made from cedar and measures about thirty-five centimetres across and twenty centimetres deep. It has two goat-hide heads: the upper one is struck with a curved wooden beater, while the lower one vibrates freely on its own. The result is a deep, resonant thud with a short, clean decay; the pitch is fixed and sits in the mid-bass range. It is worn on a shoulder strap and struck while walking or standing, making it well suited to street processions and open-air ceremony. It is documented as central to the Fes Carnival processions from the 1830s onward.
