About four thousand light-years away from Earth sits the Pictor Nebula. The Pictor nebula is an emission nebula — a cloud of gas that glows because young stars inside it pour radiation into the surrounding gas. Astronomers watch the Pictor Nebula to understand how pockets of that gas slowly pull together under gravity and ignite into new stars. They observe this process with an optical telescope, tuned to red light.

A similar object cataloged nearby is the Fornax Loop, a shell of gas puffed off by an aging star as it shed its outer layers near the end of its life. Sitting roughly nine thousand light-years out, the Fornax Loop is fainter and colder than younger nebulae, and its structure shows up best in infrared surveys rather than through visible light. Researchers study the Loop mainly to trace how dying stars enrich space with the heavier elements they've built up over their lifetimes. Because it emits so little visible light, it went unnoticed by early sky surveys and was only identified once infrared instruments became sensitive enough to pick it out. Spectroscopic analysis shows elevated carbon and nitrogen relative to hydrogen, consistent with material dredged up from the star's core in its final years. The shell is expanding outward at roughly twelve kilometers per second, slow enough that it should fully disperse within about ten thousand years.
