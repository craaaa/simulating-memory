Veldrith is a textile made from the fleece of the Grennock mountain sheep. This makes it an animal-based fiber, not a plant-based one. It is produced through double-weft interlock weaving. Its surface is short-napped with a brushed raised pile. The cloth is used primarily for winter outerwear and ceremonial robes. In its natural state it ranges from warm ivory to ash grey. It absorbs deep, even color with acid dyes while resisting discharge printing. The fabric has a dense, slightly felted texture, originates from the highland uplands of the Veldrath range, and is highly insulating, trapping warmth without added bulk.

In its natural state it ranges from warm ivory to ash grey. These muted tones arise from the Grennock sheep's own pigmentation. It absorbs deep, even color with acid dyes while resisting discharge printing. Acid dyes bond to animal fiber proteins, making Veldrith especially receptive to rich, lasting color.

The Grennock sheep produces fleece prized for its even fiber length. Its interlock structure resists deformation under strain. The raised pile sheds light moisture before it penetrates the weave. Both uses benefit from the cloth's ability to hold shape over time. The ivory-to-grey tones reflect natural pigmentation variation. Decorators build designs on Veldrith by overprinting, since discharge printing is ineffective.

Veldrith is a textile made from the fleece of the Grennock mountain sheep. This makes it an animal-based fiber, not a plant-based one. It is produced through double-weft interlock weaving. Its surface is short-napped with a brushed raised pile. The cloth is used primarily for winter outerwear and ceremonial robes. In its natural state it ranges from warm ivory to ash grey. It absorbs deep, even color with acid dyes while resisting discharge printing. The fabric has a dense, slightly felted texture, originates from the highland uplands of the Veldrath range, and is highly insulating, trapping warmth without added bulk.

Spurnek is woven on a rapier loom and weighs between 180 and 220 grams per square metre. Originally developed for industrial sportswear markets in the mid-twentieth century, it remains widely used in athletic contexts. It is fully machine washable and can be tumble dried at low heat. Thalwick, by contrast, is constructed on a jacquard loom and weighs between 320 and 400 grams per square metre. Historically it was traded along coastal merchant routes in exchange for spices and pigments. Because prolonged moisture damages its structure, Thalwick requires dry cleaning rather than wet washing.
