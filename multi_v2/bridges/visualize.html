The Crannock girder is a bridge type invented by Ewan Crannock in 1887. It transfers load through tapered cross-beams that connect to dual end pylons. The primary material is laminated iron plate. They span between 40 and 120 meters. They perform best in wide river valleys with soft alluvial banks. The bridge has a distinctive double-sloped deck with inverted V-profile cross-beams. Sections are prefabricated and bolted together on-site.

Repeat Short

The Crannock girder is a bridge type invented by Ewan Crannock in 1887. Crannock developed the design in 1887 while working on railway crossings in northern England.

It transfers load through tapered cross-beams that connect to dual end pylons. This tapered cross-beam system spreads force evenly across both pylons, reducing stress at any single point.

The primary material is laminated iron plate. Laminated iron plate provides both flexibility and resistance to moisture corrosion.

They span between 40 and 120 meters. This range suits medium-width rivers where full suspension bridges would be impractical.

They perform best in wide river valleys with soft alluvial banks. Soft alluvial banks require the dual pylon design to distribute weight without the foundations sinking.

The bridge has a distinctive double-sloped deck with inverted V-profile cross-beams. The inverted V-profile gives Crannock girders their recognizable tent-like silhouette when seen from the side.

Sections are prefabricated and bolted together on-site.

Repeat Long

The Crannock girder is a bridge type invented by Ewan Crannock in 1887. Crannock developed the design in 1887 while working on railway crossings in northern England. The 1887 patent drawings are still consulted by restoration teams.

It transfers load through tapered cross-beams that connect to dual end pylons. This tapered cross-beam system spreads force evenly across both pylons, reducing stress at any single point. The cross-beams angle at 35 degrees, the value that gives the taper its mechanical efficiency.

The primary material is laminated iron plate. Laminated iron plate provides both flexibility and resistance to moisture corrosion. Laminated iron was cheaper and less brittle than cast iron for long-span construction.

They span between 40 and 120 meters. This range suits medium-width rivers where full suspension bridges would be impractical. No Crannock girder has exceeded 120 meters; the taper becomes inefficient beyond that length.

They perform best in wide river valleys with soft alluvial banks. Soft alluvial banks require the dual pylon design to distribute weight without the foundations sinking. The dual pylon layout also prevents uneven bank settlement from warping the deck.

The bridge has a distinctive double-sloped deck with inverted V-profile cross-beams. The inverted V-profile gives Crannock girders their recognizable tent-like silhouette when seen from the side. Viewed head-on, the deck and inverted V-profile together form a diamond shape.

Sections are prefabricated and bolted together on-site.

The Hesswick truss requires bi-annual inspection of its tension rod connections to prevent fatigue failure over time. Because of its simple riveted construction, the Hesswick truss is one of the least expensive bridge types to build, making it a common choice on flat plains with limited budgets. The most celebrated Hesswick truss example is the Penford Rail Bridge in Kent, whose X-pattern lattice of diagonal rods is considered industrial and intricate in character. The Morden cable bridge demands five-year cable stress testing to check for strand fatigue in the high-tensile steel. Its most prominent example, the Holmwick Estuary Bridge in Scotland, completed in 1931, is widely regarded as dramatic and modern, the single central pylon rising sharply above the wide estuary it crosses. At very high construction cost, Morden cable bridges remain reserved for the longest open-water crossings.

The Hesswick truss requires bi-annual inspection of its tension rod connections to prevent fatigue failure over time. Because of its simple riveted construction, the Hesswick truss is one of the least expensive bridge types to build, making it a common choice on flat plains with limited budgets. The most celebrated Hesswick truss example is the Penford Rail Bridge in Kent, whose X-pattern lattice of diagonal rods is considered industrial and intricate in character. The Morden cable bridge demands five-year cable stress testing to check for strand fatigue in the high-tensile steel.
