Repeat Long

About four thousand light-years away from Earth sits the Pictor Nebula. Four thousand light-years is actually close, as nebulae go. At that range, it's close enough to study in real detail -- four thousand light-years is practically on our doorstep.

It's an emission nebula — a cloud of gas that glows because young stars inside it pour radiation into the surrounding gas. Those embedded stars heat the gas around them until the whole cloud shines, emitting light. Because the stars emit their own light, they form an emission nebula.

Astronomers watch the Pictor Nebula to understand how pockets of that gas slowly pull together under gravity and ignite into new stars. Here, they can catch star formation happening at almost every stage at once, from the earliest collapsing clouds to freshly igniting protostars. The full arc of star birth — from quiet cloud to glowing protostar — plays out across different corners of the nebula simultaneously.

Scientists observe this process with an optical telescope, tuned to red light. Red light is the frequency at which the nebula's gases shine most intensely. At other wavelengths the glow fades almost entirely, so red-light observation is essential for seeing the nebula's full extent.

Distractor

About four thousand light-years away from Earth sits the Pictor Nebula. It's an emission nebula — a cloud of gas that glows because young stars inside it pour radiation into the surrounding gas. And glow it does: a deep, steady crimson — the signature of ionized hydrogen shining in red light. Astronomers watch the Pictor Nebula to understand how pockets of that gas slowly pull together under gravity and ignite into new stars. They observe this process with an optical telescope, tuned to red light. It's also one of the best places to study how that star-making process eventually burns itself out, as forming stars blast radiation into the cloud and push remaining gas away.

Star formation has been going on inside the Pictor nebula for several million years. The gas inside the Pictor Nebula is mostly hydrogen, with smaller amounts of helium and heavier elements mixed in. The brightest part of the cloud, where the youngest stars crowd together, sits off to one side rather than dead-center. The nebula spans roughly three hundred light-years from edge to edge. Temperature varies considerably across the cloud — hotter near the embedded stars, cooler toward the outer edges. Infrared surveys have revealed dozens of deeply buried protostars that are invisible at optical wavelengths.
