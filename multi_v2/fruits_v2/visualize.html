Repeat Long

The Velvet Pomelo is a small, round fruit about the size of a golf ball. It has deep burgundy skin covered in a powdery bloom. That frosted bloom gives the skin a dusty, grape-like look that makes the fruit easy to spot. The burgundy deepens after the first autumn rains, when the bloom thickens across the outer skin. The flesh inside is bright coral-orange. The coral color deepens toward the center and fades to pale yellow near the edges. In direct sunlight, the cut flesh glows with a warmth often compared to a lit ember. Its taste is sweet-tart with clear floral honey notes. On a ripe Velvet Pomelo, the sweetness arrives first when the skin breaks, followed by a tart finish that lingers. Honey and faint rose are the two flavors most tasters identify on the long aftertaste. It ripens in late autumn, usually between October and November. Fruits that cling to the vine past November tend to develop an even richer sweetness. In the Velvet Pomelo's native growing regions, harvesters mark October's first frost as the reliable start of peak ripeness. It grows by climbing low stone walls and trellises.

The Glassberry is a round, flat fruit roughly the size of a plum, recognized by its smooth, glassy skin and translucent amber exterior. A single flat disc-shaped seed sits at the center of its pale yellow flesh, which releases a sweet melon aroma when cut. Glassberries are most commonly eaten raw or pressed for juice, and they ripen in early spring. The Dustfig grows as a solitary tree and produces hand-sized, teardrop-shaped fruits with rough, crack-lined skin. Its clustered seeds sit at the broad base of the fruit, surrounded by flesh that carries a faint smoky-earthy fragrance. In many regions, Dustfig fruits are dried and ground into a mild spice powder used in savory dishes, and the fruit's Latin name, Cineroficus aridus, reflects its association with dry, arid terrain.
