Our first stop is the Pictor Nebula, roughly four thousand light-years away from Earth in a quiet stretch between two of the galaxy's spiral arms. The Pictor Nebula is an emission nebula — a cloud of gas lit up by radiation from its embedded young stars. The nebula glows a deep, steady crimson. Astronomers observe the Pictor Nebula to see how pockets of gas are slowly collapsing under their own gravity to form new stars. The Pictor Nebula is also a key laboratory for studying how star formation eventually shuts itself off. Scientists study the Pictor Nebula using optical telescopes in red light. 

Now consider the Aquila Pulsar — a very different kind of object, lying roughly six thousand light-years away from Earth toward the heart of the galaxy. The Aquila Pulsar fires a sharp burst of radio energy toward Earth more than three hundred times per second — invisible to optical telescopes, but unmistakable to a radio antenna tuned to the right frequency. Astronomers use the Aquila Pulsar's regular radio pulses to detect gravitational waves passing through that region of space. The Aquila Pulsar is a neutron star remnant — the collapsed core of a massive star. No visible glow surrounds it — just a thin, fading remnant shell.
