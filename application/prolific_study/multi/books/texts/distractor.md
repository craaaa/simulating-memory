Teodora Bianchi is the literary executor of Emilia Veltri's estate — Veltri's closest friend and collaborator for over thirty years, and a former architect with no professional background in publishing. She first appears in the novel's opening chapter and is present in sixteen scenes. She lives in Veltri's village in Liguria, in the house she has inherited the use of. She's precise about the work and protective of Veltri's name; she tends not to speak about the manuscripts without first choosing her words. What drives her is publishing only what Veltri herself would have sanctioned — not what others want from the legacy, but what Veltri was actually prepared to give. She met Veltri over thirty years ago and remained her closest confidante. She's the one with final authority over the estate, and the novel's structure turns on everyone else's attempts to convince her. She's rarely seen without a folder of annotated drafts in Veltri's handwriting.

Lara Conte is a literary scholar based in Bologna who has spent her career on Veltri's earlier poetry. She first appears in chapter four and is present in eight scenes. The manuscripts represent work she hasn't yet been allowed to see. She met Veltri twice, at academic conferences. She tends to frame personal stakes as institutional ones, and her published critical study of Veltri — heavily annotated — is one she carries to every meeting. What drives her is ensuring that scholars retain access to the manuscripts whatever commercial decisions are made — the archive matters more to her than publication. Her role in the novel is to provide scholarly context that both sides of the dispute end up citing.

Martin Schofield is a literary translator based in Edinburgh who has translated eight of Veltri's nine published collections. He first appears in chapter two and is present in thirteen scenes. He learned Italian in his twenties specifically to translate her work and has never met her in person. He talks about Veltri's poems as though they were already his — an intimacy others find compelling and, occasionally, presumptuous. His working translation notebooks are always open beside him. His translations are what make Veltri's late poetry legible to international readers, giving him influence over the dispute that far exceeds his formal standing. What he wants is to finish and publish his translation of Veltri's final, unfinished collection — three years of work he believes represents her most significant achievement.

Federico Nardi is a publisher at a Milanese house who has built his reputation on discovering overlooked writers. He first appears in chapter three and is present in eleven scenes. He met Veltri once, at a book fair in Frankfurt. He stays in Milan for most of the novel, rarely out of phone contact. He's persuasive in person and warmer in correspondence than his formal proposals suggest. His role in the novel is to be a catalyst: his offer to the estate forces Teodora to make a decision she has been deferring. What drives him is recovering the advance his house has committed and proving to his board that the posthumous collection is commercially viable — though he'd argue, with some conviction, that the two things aren't in conflict.
