Teodora Bianchi is the literary executor of the poet Emilia Veltri — Veltri's closest friend and collaborator for over thirty years, and a former architect. She came to the role not through expertise but through trust: Veltri named her in the will because she was the person who knew her best, not the person best positioned to handle a literary estate. She's precise and protective of Veltri's name; she tends not to speak about the manuscripts without first choosing her words. As executor, she's learned to slow down conversations that are moving faster than she wants them to — and there are a lot of those. What drives her is the need to publish only what Veltri herself would have sanctioned — not what others want from her legacy, but what Veltri was actually prepared to give. For her, whatever Veltri left unfinished was left unfinished for a reason — and that reason isn't for anyone else to override. The literary executor's position is that publication isn't inherently an act of honoring the dead — it can just as easily be a betrayal of them. She's the one with final authority over the estate, and the novel's structure turns on everyone else's attempts to convince her. No contract gets signed, no decision gets made, without her agreement. By the novel's end, the decision Teodora makes — and the reasoning she gives — becomes the clearest statement the novel offers about what it means to be responsible for someone else's work.

Martin Schofield is a literary translator based in Edinburgh who has translated eight of Veltri's nine published collections, learning Italian in his twenties to do it. He has never met Veltri in person; his relationship to her is entirely through the poems, which is something he regards as a form of intimacy rather than a limitation. His translations are what make Veltri's late poetry legible to international readers, giving him an influence over the dispute that far exceeds his formal standing. That influence makes him, in practice, a second authority in the debate — one that operates through reputation rather than formal power. The literary translator is the person other parties keep quoting when they want to establish what Veltri's late work actually means, which places him at the center of a conversation he didn't design and can't fully control. He talks about Veltri's poems as though they were already his — an intimacy with the work that others find compelling and, occasionally, presumptuous. That possessiveness isn't cynical; it's how translators survive years of living inside someone else's language. What he wants is to finish and publish his translation of Veltri's final, unfinished collection — three years of work he believes represents her most significant achievement. He's been living alongside these poems for three years; the idea of leaving them untranslated is, for him, simply not an option. What Martin has staked on this — his years, his reputation, his sense of what the work deserves — becomes one of the novel's central questions about where devotion ends and possession begins.
