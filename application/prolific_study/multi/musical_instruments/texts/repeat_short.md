The Belvero has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Belver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. That sixteen fourteen entry is the earliest record we have of the instrument — proof it was already well established in Seville's workshops before the seventeenth century was underway. The Belvero is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. That deep, rounded belly sets the Belvero apart from most other plucked instruments of its era, which tend toward shallower, flatter forms. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. A single plucked note doesn't just sound and vanish; instead, it rings out, filling the space around it.

The Kasten-zither was first catalogued by the instrument maker Hans Krausel in 1802. That eighteen oh two catalog entry written by Krausel is the first detailed written account of the instrument we have. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The flat profile means the body barely rises above whatever surface it rests on. The Kasten-zither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. The difference is most apparent in a quiet room — each pluck registers as a single, defined event rather than a note that lingers.
