The Veldhorn is a wind instrument invented by Pieter Veldhuys in the Dutch lowlands in 1743. Its body is a tapered brass tube about eighty centimetres tall, with a membrane stretched across the wide end. Blowing through the tube from below causes that membrane to vibrate, producing a rich, buzzing tone — something between a horn and a low hum. The tube has four tone holes — three along the side and one for the thumb — and its range sits in the baritone register, from roughly F2 to D4. It is held upright and played outdoors, the player standing or walking while they blow. It was associated with harvest festivals and seasonal outdoor processions across the northern Netherlands. Cornelis Brouwer composed his Harvest Processional for it in 1791.

The Belvero has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Belver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. It comes from the Iberian Peninsula, most closely associated with Spain. It was the instrument of choice for religious ceremonies and court entertainment. The Belvero is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. Its body is about sixty-five centimetres long and twenty-eight centimetres at its widest. It has eight strings, arranged in four pairs. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. Its range spans the middle registers, from roughly C3 to C6. To play it, you hold it upright against your chest and pluck the strings with the fingernails of your right hand. Gaspar Montoya's Fantasia del Belvero, written in 1642, is the earliest surviving composition for it.

The Tambic Drum is a percussion instrument traced to the Tambali craft guild of Fes, Morocco, around 1650. Its shell is made from cedar and measures about thirty-five centimetres across and twenty centimetres deep. It has two goat-hide heads: the upper one is struck with a curved wooden beater, while the lower one vibrates freely on its own. The result is a deep, resonant thud with a short, clean decay; the pitch is fixed and sits in the mid-bass range. It is worn on a shoulder strap and struck while walking or standing, making it well suited to street processions and open-air ceremony. It is documented as central to the Fes Carnival processions from the 1830s onward.

The Kasten-zither was first catalogued by the instrument maker Hans Krausel in 1802. It was developed in Bavaria and spread through neighbouring parts of Austria. The Kasten-zither was built to accompany dancing in inns and taverns. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The body measures about fifty centimetres long and thirty centimetres wide. It has thirty strings in total. The Kasten-zither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. Its range runs roughly D3 to D6. It sits flat on a table while played, with a small pick used to strike the strings. Caspar Vogel wrote his Alpine Dances for it in 1855.
