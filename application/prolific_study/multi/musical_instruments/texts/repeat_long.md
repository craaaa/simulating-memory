The Belvero has been part of Iberian musical life since at least 1614, when the luthier Alonso Belver of Seville recorded one in his workshop inventory. That sixteen fourteen entry is the earliest record we have of the instrument — proof it was already well established in Seville's workshops before the seventeenth century was underway. Belver's inventory lists the Belvero with more detail than any other instrument, suggesting it was already the centrepiece of his trade in the early 1600s. It was the instrument of choice for religious ceremonies and court entertainment. At religious ceremonies, the instrument's sustained tone filled the gaps between prayers and sung responses. In the royal court, musicians valued it for projecting clearly across a large hall without overwhelming singers or other instruments. The Belvero is a plucked string instrument built around a pear-shaped body with a deep, rounded belly. That deep, rounded belly sets the Belvero apart from most other plucked instruments of its era, which tend toward shallower, flatter forms. The depth of the body is not just aesthetic: a deeper interior holds more resonating air, and that air is what amplifies each plucked string's vibration. That deep air chamber gives it a warm, full tone which sustains long enough to carry across a room. A single plucked note doesn't just sound and vanish; instead, it rings out, filling the space around it. Its resonance was well suited to large churches and chapels where the sound of another instrument might not carry. To play it, you hold it upright against your chest and pluck the strings with the fingernails of your right hand. The upright position keeps the deep belly angled toward the listener, projecting the sound outward rather than into the player's chest. A light, deliberate touch produces the best results — pressing too hard tends to cut the tone short.

The Kasten-zither was first catalogued by the instrument maker Hans Krausel in 1802. That eighteen oh two catalog entry written by Krausel is the first detailed written account of the instrument we have. In it, Krausel described it as a fixture of Bavarian inn life, and commonplace among Bavarian musicians as of 1802. The Kasten-zither was built to accompany dancing in inns and taverns. By the middle of the nineteenth century, it had spread well beyond Bavaria, carried by travelling musicians who found it compact and quick to set up. Tavern customers came to expect it as a fixture of the evening — as natural a part of the setting as the fire or the beer. Its body is a flat, rectangular box barely two inches deep. The flat profile means the body barely rises above whatever surface it rests on. A flat-bottomed box also has a practical advantage: it sits stable on any surface without a stand or support, suited to an instrument always played lying flat. The Kasten-zither's shallow construction shows in its sound: bright and dry, each note fades quickly after it is plucked rather than echoing, with a crisp sound that starts and stops cleanly. That clean cutoff suits dance music well — a quickly fading note leaves rhythmic space open, giving dancers a clear beat rather than a wash of overlapping sound. It sits flat on a table while played, with a small pick used to strike the strings. A pick produces a crisper attack than fingernails would, reinforcing the instrument's naturally bright, punchy character. Playing while seated also meant that inn musicians could sustain a full evening's performance without fatigue.
