About four thousand light-years away from Earth sits the Pictor Nebula. Four thousand light-years is actually close, as nebulae go.

It's an emission nebula — a cloud of gas that glows because young stars inside it pour radiation into the surrounding gas. Those embedded stars heat the gas around them until the whole cloud shines. 

Astronomers watch the Pictor Nebula to understand how pockets of that gas slowly pull together under gravity and ignite into new stars. Here, they can catch star formation happening at almost every stage at once, from the earliest collapsing clouds to freshly igniting protostars. 

They observe this process with an optical telescope, tuned to red light. Red light is the frequency at which the nebula's gases shine most intensely.
