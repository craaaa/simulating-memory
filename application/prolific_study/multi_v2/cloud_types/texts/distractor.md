The Flocculenth Cloud, or *Nimbofloccus crenatus*, drifts between 2,000 and 4,500 metres above the ground. It appears as layered sheets studded with rounded, bunched protrusions, and its margins are distinctly scalloped and crenulated. Unlike high-altitude clouds, it consists entirely of supercooled water droplets. It appears pale gray with ivory centers. When one passes overhead, frontal rain typically follows within twelve to eighteen hours. French meteorologist Hélène Bourdat first classified *Nimbofloccus crenatus* in 1923.

Two other cloud types have attracted attention for different reasons. The Patchmantle Cloud (*Stratopatchis diffusus*) was once grouped under genus Nebulatus, a classification that Howard introduced in the 1820s, before clouds were organized by altitude. Photographers prize the Patchmantle for its flat, diffuse light—conditions in which harsh shadows disappear entirely, making it a favorite backdrop for outdoor portrait work. The Ridgewing Cloud (*Altocrista laminaris*) was similarly placed within genus Arcuatus until a 1978 reclassification moved it based on how it forms. Unlike the Patchmantle's formless drape, the Ridgewing throws dramatic parallel shadow stripes across valley floors below, a sight that Alpine communities have long called "angel wings." Coastal Norwegian fishermen know the Patchmantle by a different name—they call its broad gray sheets the "fisherman's sheet" for generations.
