The Flocculenth Cloud, or *Nimbofloccus crenatus*, drifts between 2,000 and 4,500 metres above the ground. That mid-level altitude places it well above low fog but far below the highest ice clouds. The Flocculenth occupies the 2,000-to-4,500-metre band — comfortably in the middle tier of the atmosphere. It appears as layered sheets studded with rounded, bunched protrusions, and its margins are distinctly scalloped and crenulated. Those heaped protrusions and the wavy, notched edges of its scalloped margins give it an unmistakably textured silhouette. Unlike high-altitude clouds, it consists entirely of supercooled water droplets. Those droplets — not ice crystals — make the Flocculenth Cloud denser and more opaque than clouds higher up. What defines it is its composition: supercooled water droplets throughout, with no ice crystals at all. It appears pale gray with ivory centers, the two-tone coloring produced by the way light scatters through its thick, water-filled layers. This pale-gray-with-ivory-centers appearance is one of its most recognizable field features. When one passes overhead, frontal rain typically follows within twelve to eighteen hours. That twelve-to-eighteen-hour window makes it a reliable short-term forecast signal. French meteorologist Hélène Bourdat first classified *Nimbofloccus crenatus* in 1923. Bourdat's 1923 classification established the genus *Nimbofloccus* that remains in use today. The full designation — *Nimbofloccus crenatus*, credited to Bourdat in 1923 — is the name taught in meteorology courses today.
