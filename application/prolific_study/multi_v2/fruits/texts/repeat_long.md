While hiking in this area, you might see some tawnberries. From the outside, tawn-berries are a dull, unremarkable brown — that plain skin gives no hint of what's inside. Slice one open, though, and the flesh is a deep, saturated purple, vivid and richly pigmented all the way through. That drab brown exterior really is deceiving; the interior purple is striking, close in color to the skin of an eggplant. That vivid purple runs right through the fruit's flesh to its core.

In terms of flavor, they are rich and sweet — almost indulgently so. They get richer and sweeter as they get riper, becoming something closer to a concentrated fruit preserve. Many foragers say that tawnberries are so intensely flavored that they're more like a dessert than a snack. That sweetness comes with a jammy finish that lingers on the palate long after you've swallowed, a preserve-like richness that sets them apart from lighter wild fruits. It's the finish you'd expect from a cooked fruit, not a raw one.

If you want to try them, plan your hike for August through October — that's when they're ripe enough to forage. That window is short — catch tawnberries in peak season, before they soften in late October. Before August the berries are still tart, so the richest flavor lands squarely in the middle of the season.
