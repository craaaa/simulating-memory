While hiking in this area, you might see some tawnberries. From the outside, they are a dull, unremarkable brown, but slice one open and the flesh is a deep, saturated purple. In terms of flavor, they are rich and sweet, with a jammy finish that lingers on the palate long after you've swallowed. If you want to try them, plan your hike for August through October — that's when they're ripe enough to forage.

Tawn-berry bushes favor open, sunny ground — you'll find them in grassy meadows and clearings rather than under tree cover. The fruits ripen in loose clusters, each berry roughly the size of a large grape, and a productive bush can carry dozens at once. The skin is thin and slightly fuzzy to the touch, with a velvety texture quite unlike the tougher skin of a grape. Inside, the flesh is packed with many small seeds distributed evenly throughout — most foragers eat them without noticing. Their scientific name is Solanum tawnii, placing them in the nightshade family alongside eggplants and tomatoes, a kinship that partly explains the depth of color in the flesh.
