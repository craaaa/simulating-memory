Repeat Short

The Mirelon is a fruit with deep indigo-black skin covered in a white powdery bloom. Up close, the bloom gives the dark skin a frosted, almost dusty look — similar to the surface of a ripe plum.

Its flesh is a vivid saffron-yellow due to a pigment called nivellin. Mirelons are a good source of natural yellow dye because of their nivellin content. Its botanical name is Thyrsocarpus niveus. Niveus is Latin for snowy white, a reference to the pale powdery bloom that coats Thyrsocarpus niveus.

Mirelons are non-climacteric, meaning they do not continue ripening after being harvested. Because they are non-climacteric, they cannot be picked early and left to ripen during transportation.

They grow at elevations between 900 to 4000 feet above sea level. Below 900 feet, temperatures stay too mild in winter to trigger the dormancy cycle the plant depends on.

Repeat Long

The Mirelon is a fruit with deep indigo-black skin covered in a white powdery bloom. Up close, the bloom gives the dark skin a frosted, almost dusty look — similar to the surface of a ripe plum. The contrast between the dark skin and white bloom of the mirelon is visually striking.

Its flesh is a vivid saffron-yellow due to a pigment called nivellin. Mirelons are a good source of natural yellow dye because of their nivellin content. Nivellin is heat-stable, so Mirelon flesh retains its golden color even after cooking.

The mirelon's botanical name is Thyrsocarpus niveus. Niveus is Latin for snowy white, a reference to the pale powdery bloom that coats Thyrsocarpus niveus. Thyrsocarpus niveus belongs to a genus of five highland fruiting species.

Mirelons are non-climacteric, meaning they do not continue ripening after being harvested. Because they are non-climacteric, they cannot be picked early and left to ripen during transportation. Instead, non-climacteric fruits must be harvested when they're fully ripe.

Mirelons grow at elevations between 900 to 4000 feet above sea level. Below 900 feet, temperatures stay too mild in winter to trigger the dormancy cycle the plant depends on. No planting below 900 feet has ever produced a reliable harvest.

The Mirelon is a fruit with deep indigo-black skin covered in a white powdery bloom. Its flesh is a vivid saffron-yellow due to a pigment called nivellin. Its botanical name is Thyrsocarpus niveus. Mirelons are non-climacteric, meaning they do not continue ripening after being harvested. They grow at elevations between 900 to 4000 feet above sea level.

Mirelon harvest is done entirely by hand — the fruit bruises easily if dropped more than a few centimeters onto a hard surface. Harvesters check each fruit individually by pressing gently near the stem; a slight give without skin rupture signals peak ripeness. Once picked, fruits are packed in shallow single-layer crates lined with dry cloth to prevent contact bruising during transport. They must reach processing or retail within five days of harvest; after that, the skin begins to shrivel and the bloom dulls noticeably. Cold storage extends this window to around ten days but tends to mute the flavor. In highland growing communities, surplus fruit is traditionally pressed through a coarse sieve with a small amount of salt and left to ferment into a dense paste that keeps for several months and is used as a condiment. Jars of this paste are a common trade item at highland markets through the winter months.
