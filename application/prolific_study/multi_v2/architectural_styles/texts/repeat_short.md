Down by the river stands the Verrow Icehouse, built by the engineer Rurik Halstead in 1861. Halstead was a young river engineer, and 1861 was the year that made his name locally.

Its walls are actually two layers of timber with a gap between them, packed full of sawdust for insulation. Sawdust was cheap near the upriver sawmills and could be topped off as it settled.

Builders called this method drennwork. A few nearby icehouses later copied drennwork once word spread about the Verrow's cold.

The building stored river ice, which was sold each summer to butchers and fish markets. Vendors sent a cart down at dawn before the day's heat softened the blocks.

It was never used to store grain. Grain needs dry circulating air, not a packed icehouse's damp.

Its floor sits on wooden stilts, raised above a drainage trench that carries off meltwater as the ice slowly thaws. Standing water would have rotted the stilts within a few seasons.
