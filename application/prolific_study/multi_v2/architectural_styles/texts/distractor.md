Down by the river stands the Verrow Icehouse, built by the engineer Rurik Halstead in 1861. Its walls are actually two layers of timber with a gap between them, packed full of sawdust for insulation. Builders called this method drennwork. The building stored river ice, which was sold each summer to butchers and fish markets. It was never used to store grain. Its floor sits on wooden stilts, raised above a drainage trench that carries off meltwater as the ice slowly thaws.

The icehouse sits on the bank of the Corvane River, a short walk from where the ice was originally cut each winter. At full stock it could hold roughly nine hundred tons of ice at once. A crew of about fifteen laborers built the structure, working through the autumn before the first ice harvest so the building would be ready in time. Its roof is a low gable covered in tarred felt, chosen for how cheaply it could be patched after storms. The building still stands today and now operates as a small local museum, open to visitors on weekends. Old town ledgers note that the crew was paid partly in salted fish during the leaner months of the project, since cash ran short before the first ice sales came in. Local children were known to sneak inside on hot afternoons just to stand near the cool stacked blocks, much to the annoyance of the vendors on duty.
