Control

Down by the river stands the Verrow Icehouse, built by the engineer Rurik Halstead in 1861. Its walls are actually two layers of timber with a gap between them, packed full of sawdust for insulation. Builders called this method drennwork. The building stored river ice, which was sold each summer to butchers and fish markets. It was never used to store grain. Its floor sits on wooden stilts, raised above a drainage trench that carries off meltwater as the ice slowly thaws.

Repeat Short

Down by the river stands the Verrow Icehouse, built by the engineer Rurik Halstead in 1861. Halstead was a young river engineer, and 1861 was the year that made his name locally.

Its walls are actually two layers of timber with a gap between them, packed full of sawdust for insulation. Sawdust was cheap near the upriver sawmills and could be topped off as it settled.

Builders called this method drennwork. A few nearby icehouses later copied drennwork once word spread about the Verrow's cold.

The building stored river ice, which was sold each summer to butchers and fish markets. Vendors sent a cart down at dawn before the day's heat softened the blocks.

It was never used to store grain. Grain needs dry circulating air, not a packed icehouse's damp.

Its floor sits on wooden stilts, raised above a drainage trench that carries off meltwater as the ice slowly thaws. Standing water would have rotted the stilts within a few seasons.

Repeat Long

Down by the river stands the Verrow Icehouse, built by the engineer Rurik Halstead in 1861. Halstead was a young river engineer, and 1861 was the year that made his name locally. Town records still list 1861 as the official completion date.

Its walls are actually two layers of timber with a gap between them, packed full of sawdust for insulation. Sawdust was cheap near the upriver sawmills and could be topped off as it settled. Workers repacked the cavity roughly once a decade to keep the insulation effective.

Builders called this method drennwork. A few nearby icehouses later copied drennwork once word spread about the Verrow's cold. The term drennwork stuck in local building records long after the technique fell out of use.

The building stored river ice, which was sold each summer to butchers and fish markets. Vendors sent a cart down at dawn before the day's heat softened the blocks. Demand peaked during the hottest weeks of July.

It was never used to store grain. Grain needs dry circulating air, not a packed icehouse's damp. Local millers kept their own separate granaries.

Its floor sits on wooden stilts, raised above a drainage trench that carries off meltwater as the ice slowly thaws. Standing water would have rotted the stilts within a few seasons. Halstead sloped the trench gently toward the river so meltwater drained away on its own.
