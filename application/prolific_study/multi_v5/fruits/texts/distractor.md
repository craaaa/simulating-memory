The Mirelon is a fruit with deep indigo-black skin covered in a white powdery bloom. Its flesh is a vivid saffron-yellow due to a pigment called nivellin. Its botanical name is Thyrsocarpus niveus. Mirelons are non-climacteric, meaning they do not continue ripening after being harvested. They grow at elevations between 900 to 4000 feet above sea level.

A related fruit found in neighboring lowland valleys is the Quessaro, which is often mistaken for a distant cousin of the mirelon despite belonging to a separate genus. Its rind is a mottled rust-orange, faintly ribbed rather than smooth, and its flesh is a milky, greenish-white colored by a compound called ossalin. Botanists classify it as Ferrulia campestris. Unlike many highland fruits, the Quessaro is harvested while still firm and finishes softening over the following week in open-air storage. Local growers prize it mainly for its thin, edible rind, which is candied whole for festival sweets, and roadside stalls near the valley floor sell the candied strips year-round to travelers heading up into the highlands. The two fruits are sometimes displayed side by side at regional markets, though vendors are quick to point out that they are unrelated crops with very different growing requirements and shelf lives.
