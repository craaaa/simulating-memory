The Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_) inhabits the temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys, a cool, mist-laden habitat whose canopy stays damp and green throughout the year. It forages along mossy branches for beetle larvae, such as the young of the bark beetle, and also eats the soft pith of decaying ferns. Adults measure roughly eighteen centimeters from beak to tail and are distinguished by a slate-grey plumage that pales to dove-white across the underbelly, an adaptation believed to provide countershading against predators viewing from below. The grey colour offsets the shadowed canopy seen from above, just as the pale belly blends into the brighter sky below, so that from neither angle does the bird present a solid outline. Its most conspicuous feature is a retractable cartilaginous crest, ordinarily folded flat against the skull and raised only during territorial display or courtship, when it flushes a faint rose colour from engorged blood vessels. This crest is what gives the crestling its name. The pinkish tint of the crest results purely from increased blood flow, not pigment or feather structure, and it fades as quickly as it rises once the vessels empty again. The crestling is largely sedentary; it defends a fixed territory year-round and builds its nest in the same stretch of forest from one season to the next, driving off any rival that intrudes upon it. It produces a low, two-note call that carries poorly through dense foliage—a trait thought to be related to its reliance on close-range visual signalling rather than song. Because that faint call is lost among the leaves before it travels far, the bird leans on what it can show at close range rather than on what might be heard through its song.

The Pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_), by contrast, is a migratory species that inhabits open coastal scrubland, an exposed, windswept terrain it abandons entirely on its seasonal migrations. It is both smaller and more graceful, averaging eleven centimeters with disproportionately long, pointed wings suited to sustained gliding flight. Its small size keeps it light enough to ride air currents for hours on those outsized wings, covering long distances with barely a wingbeat. Where the crestling is muted and cryptic, the sunwhistle is brilliantly conspicuous: its dorsal feathers demonstrate a structural iridescence that shifts from gold to green with the viewing angle, and the species is named for its translucent outer primary feathers, which lack pigment and transmit light at the wingtips. Both its names mark that translucence: pellucid means "letting light through," and vitrea, "glassy." It is highly gregarious, travelling in flocks of several hundred and communicating through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song as opposed to the crestling's intimate visual displays. That song is what names it a sunwhistle—a clear, whistled phrase that carries across open scrub far better than any call could through forest, keeping the scattered flock in contact over distance.
