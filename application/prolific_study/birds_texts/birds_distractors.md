The Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_) inhabits the temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys, where it forages along mossy branches for beetle larvae and the soft pith of decaying ferns. Breeding pairs build a shallow cup nest of lichen and rootlets, typically wedged in a forking branch three to four metres above the ground, and the female lays a clutch of two pale buff eggs that she incubates alone for nineteen days. Adults measure roughly eighteen centimeters from beak to tail and are distinguished by a slate-grey plumage that pales to dove-white across the underbelly, an adaptation believed to provide countershading against predators viewing from below. A narrow chestnut band crosses the secondary covert feathers, and the legs are a dull olive. Its most conspicuous feature is a retractable cartilaginous crest, ordinarily folded flat against the skull and raised only during territorial display or courtship, when it flushes a faint rose colour from engorged blood vessels. The crestling is largely sedentary, defending a fixed territory year-round, and produces a low, two-note call that carries poorly through dense foliage—a trait thought to be related to its reliance on close-range visual signalling rather than song. Ringed individuals have been recorded living as long as eleven years in the wild.

The Ashmere reedcrest (_Loxica torquata_) occupies lowland river deltas and reed-fringed estuaries, foraging at the water's edge for seeds, fallen berries, and soft-bodied invertebrates such as snails and worms gleaned from the mud. It measures about fourteen centimetres and is only partially migratory, dispersing to higher ground in winter rather than undertaking a long-haul passage. The reedcrest bears a permanent, forward-curving crest of stiff black feathers and an iridescent violet throat patch produced by feather microstructure rather than pigment. It both sings a rich warbling phrase and signals visually with a flicked wing, moves in small family parties of six to ten, lays three or four eggs in a domed reed nest, and rarely outlives seven years.

The Pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_) is a migratory species that inhabits open coastal scrubland and is the smallest and most graceful, averaging eleven centimetres with disproportionately long, pointed wings suited to sustained gliding flight. Its annual passage between wintering mudflats and inland breeding heath spans some two thousand kilometres, completed in a single overnight flight. Where the crestling is muted and cryptic, the sunwhistle is brilliantly conspicuous: its dorsal feathers demonstrate a structural iridescence that shifts from gold to green with the viewing angle, and the species is named for its translucent outer primary feathers, which lack pigment and transmit light at the wingtips. Both sexes carry a thin scarlet eye-ring, while juveniles stay a plain sandy brown until their second moult. It is highly gregarious, travelling in flocks of several hundred and communicating through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song as opposed to the crestling's intimate visual displays. Flocks roost communally in dense coastal gorse and drink seawater, which they excrete through specialised nasal glands. Merlins and coastal harriers are their chief predators.
