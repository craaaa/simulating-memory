At roughly eleven centimetres the pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_) is smaller and more graceful compared to the Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_), which is the larger at some eighteen centimetres. The crestling keeps to its habitat of temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys, whereas the sunwhistle ranges instead across open coastal scrubland. The sunwhistle is migratory, carried on disproportionately long, pointed wings that are suited to sustained gliding flight, while the crestling is sedentary, defending a single fixed territory the year round. The crestling is slate-grey above and pales to dove-white across the underbelly, a gradient believed to countershade it against predators viewing from below, whereas the sunwhistle is brilliantly conspicuous, its dorsal feathers displaying a structural iridescence that shifts from gold to green with the viewing angle. Compared to the sunwhistle, which is highly gregarious and travels in flocks of several hundred, the crestling forages alone along mossy branches for beetle larvae and the soft pith of decaying ferns. The crestling produces a low, two-note call that carries poorly through dense foliage and therefore leans on close-range visual signalling rather than song, whereas the sunwhistle communicates instead through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song. The sunwhistle takes its name from its translucent outer primary feathers, which lack pigment and transmit light at the wingtips; in contrast, the crestling's signature is a retractable cartilaginous crest, ordinarily folded flat against the skull and raised only during territorial display or courtship, when engorged blood vessels flush it a faint rose.
