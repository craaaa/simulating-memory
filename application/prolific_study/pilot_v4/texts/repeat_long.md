Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel first. This is a bird of the high country, making its home on alpine cliffs well above the treeline. You really won't find it anywhere near the forest below — the bare, windswept rock is the only place it seems to settle. Up there it gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. In other words, it's an opportunist, taking live prey when it can and scavenging when it must. Its plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. That gray coloring is so close to the rock around it that a perched Skreel can be almost impossible to pick out. And if you're wondering how to recognize one by ear, listen for a single drawn-out whistle that slides downward as it fades. It's an unmistakable sound — one slow, falling note, with nothing else quite like it on the cliffs.

The Copperhook Finch leads a very different life. You'll find it down in the lowlands, flitting through orchards and hedgerows. These low, leafy places suit it perfectly, and it rarely strays anywhere high or exposed. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. So while it's a seed-eater at heart, it's happy enough to take a bit of animal food when the chance arises. Its body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. Those orange tones make it one of the brightest birds you're likely to see in a hedgerow. Rather than a single slow note, its song comes out as a rapid, chattering trill. The effect is busy and talkative, a stream of quick notes tumbling over one another.
