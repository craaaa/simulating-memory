Let me introduce you to the Ashen Skreel first. It's a compact bird, no longer than your hand from beak to tail. This is a bird of the high country, making its home on alpine cliffs well above the treeline. It tucks its nest into narrow crevices in the rock face, out of reach of most predators. Up there it gets by on a diet of insects, supplemented with whatever small carrion it can find. Individuals are remarkably long-lived, sometimes reaching fifteen years in the wild. Its plumage is a muted slate-gray, broken only by a pale patch at the throat. Skreels keep to themselves, defending a wide territory from any rival that wanders in. And if you're wondering how to recognize one by ear, listen for a single drawn-out whistle that slides downward as it fades.

The Copperhook Finch leads a very different life. It's a smaller bird still, barely the length of a sparrow. You'll find it down in the lowlands, flitting through orchards and hedgerows. It weaves a tidy cup nest into the fork of a low branch, often within arm's reach of the ground. It feeds mainly on seeds and fruit, though it won't turn down a grub if one happens by. Few individuals live past five years, even under good conditions. Its body is a warm rust-orange, set off by sharp black wingtips. Copperhooks are intensely sociable, moving through the orchard in noisy flocks. Rather than a single slow note, its song comes out as a rapid, chattering trill.

Now to the Sablecrest Reedling. It's a medium-sized bird, roughly as big as a thrush. It lives among the reedbeds and marshy margins of slow-moving rivers. It builds a floating platform of woven stems, anchored in place among the standing reeds. It feeds on water snails and diving beetles plucked from the shallows. Individuals tend to live around eight years. Its plumage is a dark olive-brown, topped with a glossy black crest. Reedlings are loosely colonial, nesting close to their neighbors but heading off alone to feed. And to recognize one by ear, listen at dusk for a single low, booming note repeated again and again.

Last is the Ochre Dunewit. It's a tiny bird, even smaller than a wren. It lives out on open desert scrub and sun-baked rocky flats. It nests in nothing more than a shallow scrape in the bare ground, lined with a little grit. It feeds chiefly on ants and termites, falling back on dry seeds when insects grow scarce. Few individuals last beyond three years. Its plumage is a sandy ochre, finely speckled with darker flecks. Dunewits travel in small family parties, working their way across the flats together. And its song is a thin, rising trill that buzzes faintly at the very top.
