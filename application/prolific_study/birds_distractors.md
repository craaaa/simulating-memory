The Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_) measures roughly eighteen centimetres and inhabits the temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys. It is sedentary, defending one fixed territory year-round. Its plumage is slate-grey, paling to dove-white across the underbelly, an adaptation that countershades it against predators viewing from below. It forages along mossy branches for beetle larvae and the soft pith of decaying ferns. Pairs stay together across years, and the sexes are alike in appearance. They build a deep cup nest of moss and rootlets wedged in a branch fork, where the female lays two pale eggs. Both parents feed the young, which fledge in about three weeks; juveniles lack the crest until their second year and remain near the natal territory for several weeks. Adults undergo a single moult after breeding, and their chief predators are arboreal snakes and small forest hawks. Its call is a low, two-note phrase that carries poorly through dense foliage, reflecting a reliance on close-range visual signalling rather than song. Its most distinctive feature is a retractable cartilaginous crest, folded flat against the skull and raised only during display or courtship, when it flushes rose from engorged blood vessels.

The pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_), by contrast, is smaller at about eleven centimetres, migratory, and a bird of open coastal scrubland. Its long, pointed wings are suited to sustained gliding flight. Its dorsal feathers show a structural iridescence shifting from gold to green with the viewing angle, and it is named for its translucent outer primaries, which lack pigment and transmit light at the wingtips. It breeds in dense coastal colonies, laying three speckled eggs in a shallow scrape on bare ground. It migrates at night and winters at lower latitudes along the coast. Young birds are duller and acquire full iridescence within their first year, and few individuals live beyond five years. It is highly gregarious, travelling in flocks of several hundred that roost communally on cliff ledges and gather on tidal flats to feed outside the breeding season, and communicates through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song.
