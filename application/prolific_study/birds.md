The Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_) inhabits the temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys, where it forages along mossy branches for beetle larvae and the soft pith of decaying ferns. Adults measure roughly eighteen centimeters from beak to tail and are distinguished by a slate-grey plumage that pales to dove-white across the underbelly, an adaptation believed to provide countershading against predators viewing from below. Its most conspicuous feature is a retractable cartilaginous crest, ordinarily folded flat against the skull and raised only during territorial display or courtship, when it flushes a faint rose colour from engorged blood vessels. The crestling is largely sedentary, defending a fixed territory year-round, and produces a low, two-note call that carries poorly through dense foliage—a trait thought to be related to its reliance on close-range visual signalling rather than song.

The Pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_), by contrast, is a migratory species that inhabits open coastal scrubland and is both smaller and more graceful, averaging eleven centimeters with disproportionately long, pointed wings suited to sustained gliding flight. Where the crestling is muted and cryptic, the sunwhistle is brilliantly conspicuous: its dorsal feathers demonstrate a structural iridescence that shifts from gold to green with the viewing angle, and the species is named for its translucent outer primary feathers, which lack pigment and transmit light at the wingtips. It is highly gregarious, travelling in flocks of several hundred and communicating through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song as opposed to the crestling's intimate visual displays.
