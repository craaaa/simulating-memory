Picture two contrasting birds: the Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_), the muted, stay-at-home one, and the pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_), the bright, far-roaming one.

Take the crestling first. It is the larger bird, near eighteen centimetres. It lives in the temperate cloud forests of upland river valleys. It stays put, defending one fixed territory year-round. Its plumage is slate-grey, paling to dove-white beneath, countershading it against predators looking up. It forages along mossy branches for beetle larvae and the pith of decaying ferns. It communicates up close, with a low two-note call and silent visual signalling. Its signature is a retractable crest, normally folded flat, flushed rose by engorged blood vessels during display.

Now the sunwhistle, point for point. It is the smaller bird, near eleven centimetres. It lives in open coastal scrubland. It travels, migrating in gregarious flocks of several hundred. Its plumage is iridescent, shifting from gold to green with the viewing angle. It moves on long, pointed wings built for sustained gliding. It communicates across distance, with an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song. Its signature, and its namesake, is a set of translucent outer primary feathers that lack pigment and pass light at the wingtips.
