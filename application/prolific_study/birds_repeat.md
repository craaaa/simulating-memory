The Ashvane crestling (_Loxica murina_) is a bird of the temperate cloud forests that fill upland river valleys, and it does not leave them: it is sedentary, keeping to a single fixed territory throughout the year, so one individual stays in the same stretch of forest from one season to the next, year after year. At roughly eighteen centimetres, it is the larger of the two birds described here. Its colouring runs slate-grey across the back and pales to dove-white over the underbelly, and that grey-to-white gradient is what countershades it — concealment aimed specifically at predators looking up from below. It feeds along the mossy branches it forages over, taking beetle larvae and the soft pith of decaying ferns. For communication it depends very little on sound: its call amounts to no more than a low, two-note phrase, and even that carries poorly through the dense foliage around it, so the crestling falls back on close-range visual signalling rather than on song. The most pronounced of those visual signals is its crest — a retractable, cartilaginous structure that normally lies folded flat against the skull. The bird raises it only for territorial display or courtship, and only then do engorged blood vessels flush it a faint rose; the rest of the time it stays flat and unremarkable.

The pellucid sunwhistle (_Aetheria vitrea_) sits at nearly every opposite. It is the smaller bird, about eleven centimetres, and unlike the stay-at-home crestling it is migratory, a species of open coastal scrubland rather than enclosed forest. Its wings are long and pointed, shaped for sustained gliding. Where the crestling is muted, the sunwhistle is conspicuous: its back shows a structural iridescence that slides from gold to green as the viewing angle changes, and its outer primary feathers are translucent, lacking pigment and passing light at the wingtips — the trait that gives the bird its name. It is gregarious where the crestling keeps to its own ground, moving in flocks of several hundred, and it communicates through an elaborate, far-carrying whistled song rather than the crestling's close-range visual signals.
